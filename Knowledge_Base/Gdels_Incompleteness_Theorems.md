# Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems
## Overview
Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems are two [[Theorem]]s in [[Mathematical Logic]] that establish fundamental limitations on the power of formal systems. The theorems were proven by [[Kurt Gödel]] in 1931 and have had a significant impact on the development of [[Mathematics]], [[Philosophy]], and [[Computer Science]]. The first theorem states that any [[Formal System]] that is powerful enough to describe basic [[Arithmetic]] is either [[Incomplete]] or [[Inconsistent]]. The second theorem states that if a formal system is consistent, it cannot prove its own consistency.

## Concept Map
* [[Formal System]]: a set of [[Axiom]]s and [[Rule]]s for deducing [[Theorem]]s
* [[Incompleteness]]: the existence of statements that cannot be proved or disproved within a formal system
* [[Inconsistency]]: the existence of a statement that can be both proved and disproved within a formal system
* [[Kurt Gödel]]: Austrian-American mathematician and logician who proved the Incompleteness Theorems
* [[Mathematical Logic]]: the study of the principles of [[Mathematics]] using [[Logic]] and [[Formal System]]s
* [[Peano Axioms]]: a set of [[Axiom]]s for [[Arithmetic]] that are often used to demonstrate the Incompleteness Theorems
* [[Turing Machine]]: a mathematical model of computation that is often used to study the limits of computation and the implications of the Incompleteness Theorems

# Internal Energy

## Overview

Internal energy is a thermodynamic property of a system that represents the total energy contained within the system, including both kinetic energy and potential energy of the particles that make up the system. It is a measure of the total energy of a system, including the energy associated with the motion of its particles and the energy associated with the interactions between its particles.

Internal energy is a state function, meaning that it depends only on the current state of the system and not on the path by which the system reached that state. It is a fundamental concept in thermodynamics and is used to describe the behavior of systems in various fields, including physics, chemistry, and engineering.

### Mathematical Description

Internal energy (U) is a function of the system's temperature (T), volume (V), and number of particles (N). It can be expressed mathematically as:

U = U(T, V, N)

### Types of Internal Energy

- [[Kinetic Energy]]: the energy associated with the motion of particles within the system
- [[Potential Energy]]: the energy associated with the interactions between particles within the system
- [[Chemical Energy]]: the energy associated with the chemical bonds between particles within the system

### Internal Energy and the First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics states that the change in internal energy (ΔU) of a system is equal to the heat added to the system (Q) minus the work done by the system (W):

ΔU = Q - W

### Internal Energy and the Second Law of Thermodynamics

The second law of thermodynamics states that the change in entropy (ΔS) of a system is related to the change in internal energy (ΔU) and the heat added to the system (Q):

ΔS = ΔU / T + Q / T

### Internal Energy and the Third Law of Thermodynamics

The third law of thermodynamics states that as the temperature of a system approaches absolute zero, the internal energy of the system approaches a minimum value.

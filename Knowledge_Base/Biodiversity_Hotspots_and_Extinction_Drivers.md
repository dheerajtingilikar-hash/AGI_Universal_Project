# Biodiversity Hotspots and Extinction Drivers

## Overview

Biodiversity hotspots are regions with exceptionally high levels of endemism, which is the presence of species found nowhere else on Earth. These areas are often characterized by unique geological features, such as islands, mountain ranges, or tropical rainforests, that have allowed species to evolve independently over millions of years.

According to [[Conservation International]], biodiversity hotspots are defined as areas with:

* At least 1,500 species of flowering plants (vascular plants) per 10,000 square kilometers
* At least 70% of the plant species found in these areas are endemic
* Habitat loss and degradation are major threats to the survival of the species found in these areas

## Extinction Drivers

Extinction drivers are the factors that contribute to the decline and eventual extinction of species. Some of the main extinction drivers include:

* [[Overexploitation]]: The excessive use of resources, such as hunting, fishing, and logging, that can lead to population decline and extinction.
* [[Pollution]]: The release of pollutants, such as pesticides, herbicides, and industrial waste, that can harm species and their habitats.
* [[Invasive Species]]: Non-native species that can outcompete native species for resources and habitat, leading to population decline and extinction.
* [[Climate Change]]: Changes in temperature and precipitation patterns that can alter species' habitats and make it difficult for them to adapt.
